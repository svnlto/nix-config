# Chapter 4: One-to-Ones

## Core Idea

Weekly one-to-ones are the highest-leverage recurring activity a manager
has; run them as the direct report's meeting — prepared, private, and
focused on the person rather than status updates.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Contracting**: a structured set of questions worked through in your
  first one-to-one so both sides openly state what they expect, want, and
  need from the relationship.
  - When to use: the first one-to-one with each report; revisit as needs
    evolve.
  - How: both people answer each question about the other (it's
    two-way). Optionally send questions ahead for higher-quality
    answers. The five questions:
    1. Which areas would you like the most support with?
    2. How would you like to receive feedback and support?
    3. What could be a challenge of us working together?
    4. How might we know if the support I'm offering isn't going well?
    5. How confidential is the content of our meetings?

- **The 70% Rule ("It's their meeting, not yours")**: aim to get the
  direct report doing about 70% of the talking.
  - When to use: every regular one-to-one.
  - How: "keep the thought bubble over their head" — ask leading
    questions, nudge direction, listen. When you want to solve their
    problem, ask another question and let them reach the conclusion.

- **Note-and-Action System**: a shared private document per report plus
  a recurring to-do with two subtasks.
  - When to use: every one-to-one, to ensure follow-through.
  - How: keep a rolling shared agenda; write notes as bullets, actions
    in bold; review actions at the end; recurring to-do has two
    subtasks — (1) prepare notes ahead of meeting, (2) put actions in
    to-do list.

## Key Concepts

- **Leverage**: a small investment of time producing disproportionately
  large output; weekly one-to-ones are one of the best places to find
  it.
- **Cadence**: every report, every week, same time and place, in a
  private room — predictability signals you're reliably there for them.
- **Rolling agenda**: a shared document both parties add to over the
  week so there's always material to discuss.
- **Leading questions**: open prompts that stimulate discussion whether
  or not you know the answer ("How are you feeling about the June
  deadline?").
- **Silence is golden**: leaving pauses unfilled lets reports surface
  the issues that really matter to them.
- **Andon Cord**: a metaphorical stop-cord (from Toyota's production
  lines, via Mike Rother's "Toyota Kata") either party can pull to halt
  an uncomfortable topic and ask why.
- **Confidentiality default**: an explicit agreement on whether meeting
  content can be relayed to colleagues or upward to your own boss.

## Mental Models

- Think of the first one-to-one as signing a contract, like the sales
  deal overheard on the street — agree up front what each side needs so
  the relationship starts on the right foot.
- Use leading questions when you want depth; you don't need to know the
  answer for the question to work.
- Think of yourself as guide, not therapist — refer serious personal or
  mental-health issues to HR or a qualified third party; never diagnose.
- Use the Andon Cord when a topic is too personal, too off-topic, or
  needs someone better placed: stop, enquire, resolve, resume.

## Anti-patterns

- **Status-update meetings**: walking a checklist of in-flight tasks;
  boring, doesn't develop the relationship, and the info lives in
  tickets anyway — keep updates to a minimum.
- **Manager dominates the talking**: pontificating or directing instead
  of listening; breaks the 70% rule.
- **Suggesting answers for a struggling report**: proposing support
  areas they then just agree with; give airtime instead of filling the
  gap.
- **Public or shifting one-to-ones**: holding them near desks or in
  cafes, or moving them around; risks eavesdropping and erodes the safe,
  predictable touchpoint.
- **Becoming their therapist**: letting sessions become venting or deep
  personal-issue talks you're untrained for; be supportive but refer
  elsewhere.
- **Not acting on assigned actions**: raising items and never following
  through; a common failure that damages trust.

## Worked Example

Walking to the office before their first "real" one-to-ones, the new
manager overhears two salespeople discussing a signed contract — amends,
walked-back pricing, a QBR, year-end re-evaluation of terms. The insight
lands: what if you had a contract with your direct reports about what
each side needs? That becomes the contracting exercise. In the first
one-to-one, manager and report work through the five questions about each
other. One real result: a former peer who became a report worried it
felt like a demotion, so they agreed the report would keep most of their
existing decision-making autonomy — surfaced and resolved by question
three (challenges of working together) before it could poison the new
reporting line.

## Key Takeaways

1. Hold a one-to-one with every report every week, same time and place,
   in a private room — protect the cadence.
2. Use contracting for the first meeting: work both ways through the
   five questions to set expectations candidly from day one.
3. Make it their meeting — aim for them to do 70% of the talking, ask
   leading questions, and let silences do work.
4. Keep a shared rolling agenda; both of you add items through the week.
5. Minimize status updates; spend the time on personal development,
   deep dives, coaching, and trust-building topics.
6. Write notes as bullets with actions in bold, review actions at the
   end, and actually put your own actions on your to-do list.
7. You are not a therapist — sense distress, refer to HR or a
   professional, and never diagnose.
