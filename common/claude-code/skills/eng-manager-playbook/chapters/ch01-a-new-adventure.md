# Chapter 1: A New Adventure

## Core Idea

In your first week as a new manager, don't rush to act. Instead
systematically gather three perspectives on your team — your own, your
manager's, and the team's — and overlay them to reveal where the real
problems live.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Snapshot (three views of the team)**: Capture the current state
  of the team from three vantage points so you can compare them, exactly
  like a system snapshot you restore from.
  - When to use: Your first week (or first weeks) in a new management
    role, before you have your own long-term view of the team.
  - How: Gather (1) your own observations from working alongside the
    team, (2) your manager's observations, and (3) your team's
    observations, one report at a time. Write everything down, then
    overlay the three as a Venn diagram.

- **The Snapshot Venn diagram (four intersecting zones)**: Overlay the
  three views and read each region as a distinct class of problem.
  - When to use: Once you have collected all three views and want to
    turn raw notes into an action list.
  - How: Classify each observation into one of four zones — Alignment
    (all three agree), Poor communication downward (you + manager know,
    team doesn't), Poor communication upward (you + team know, manager
    doesn't), and False beliefs (manager + team agree, but you disagree
    from outside experience).

- **Introductory one-to-ones (team and manager)**: Structured first
  conversations to seed the snapshot.
  - When to use: Week one, with every direct report and with your own
    manager.
  - How: Ask for 30 minutes each, keep it informal and private, use
    open-ended questions, let them do most of the talking, take notes,
    and repeat back any actions you'll take for confirmation.

## Key Concepts

- **Snapshot**: The recorded state of your team's reality at the moment
  you join, assembled from three separate views.
- **Alignment**: Observations you, your team, and your manager all
  share — the settled common ground, positive or negative.
- **Poor communication downward**: You and your manager see something
  the team is unaware of or disagrees with.
- **Poor communication upward**: You and your team see something your
  manager is unaware of.
- **False beliefs**: Your manager and team agree on something your own
  outside experience tells you is wrong.
- **Impostor syndrome**: The internal sense of being a fraud despite
  being qualified — common and normal for new managers.
- **Flow**: A mental state (Csíkszentmihályi, 1975) of fully immersed,
  enjoyable concentration; "the zone" that makers rely on.
- **Maker's schedule vs manager's schedule**: Paul Graham's distinction
  that makers need half-day blocks while managers work in hourly slots.
- **Action list**: The prioritized output of the snapshot, sorted into
  items to raise with the team versus with your manager.

## Mental Models

- Think of yourself as a management application freshly deployed to
  production with no backup — so your first job is to create the
  snapshot you'll later restore your understanding from.
- Use open-ended questions to "keep the thought bubble over their head
  at all times" — let information surface naturally rather than
  interrogating.
- When scheduling anyone on a maker's schedule, protect their half-day
  blocks; batch your meetings toward the edges of their day.
- Read disagreement as a communication signal, not just a fact dispute:
  where views diverge tells you which direction communication has
  broken down.

## Anti-patterns

- **Acting on week-one observations before you have your own view**:
  You've only just started, so you must lean on others' views first
  rather than assuming you already understand the team.
- **Meeting the team only as a group**: Group hellos don't create the
  safe, private space needed for honest disclosure — meet everyone
  one-to-one.
- **Using email or chat to introduce yourself**: The founding
  relationship should form in person or on video, not over text.
- **Dropping meetings into makers' calendars at your own convenience**:
  It shatters their flow; ask each person for their ideal time instead.
- **Trying to fix everything you discover immediately**: The snapshot
  seeds discussions and investigations, not instant solutions.

## Worked Example

A new Infrastructure Engineering Manager spends week one meeting each
report for 30 informal minutes, asking open questions like "What's the
one thing nobody wants to speak up about?" and "What could I help you
with?" One theme recurs: the team feels understaffed for its committed
roadmap. Separately, the manager's one-to-one surfaces a different
concern — the team ships a high number of production bugs relative to
peers, something the team itself seems unaware of.

Overlaying the three views: the understaffing sits in Poor
communication upward (team and new manager see it, the boss doesn't).
The production bugs sit in Poor communication downward (boss and new
manager see it, team doesn't). A monthly, manual, failure-prone deploy
process that both team and boss consider "fine" — but which the new
manager, coming from a shop that deployed daily — flags as a False
belief. These sort straight into the action list: raise understaffing
upward with the manager, open the bug conversation downward with the
team, and challenge the deploy false belief with outside evidence.

## Key Takeaways

1. Book a recurring, private, hour-long weekly one-to-one with every
   report and with your manager during week one — regular cadence
   matters more than the first agenda.
2. In first meetings, ask open-ended questions and let the other person
   talk; capture themes in writing and confirm actions back to them.
3. Build the three-view snapshot before drawing conclusions; your own
   view isn't reliable yet.
4. Sort snapshot findings into the four Venn zones, then bucket them
   into "talk with team" and "talk with manager" action items.
5. If the action list is huge, prioritize by impact so it doesn't
   overwhelm you.
6. Keep your calendar and your captured notes organized from day one —
   you can't run this from memory.
