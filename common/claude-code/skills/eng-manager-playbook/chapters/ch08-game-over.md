# Chapter 8: Game Over

## Core Idea

People will always leave your team — voluntarily or because you make them —
and a good manager gets comfortable with normal turnover, facilitates good
departures gracefully, fights selectively for the right people to stay, and
uses a fair, evidenced Performance Improvement Plan to resolve genuine poor
performance.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Voluntary vs Involuntary Departure (regrettable vs non-regrettable)**: The
  two camps every departure falls into.
  - When to use: Categorizing any exit to decide your response.
  - How: Voluntary means they leave of their own accord — ensure it's for
    positive reasons and facilitate it. Involuntary means you're ending
    employment, usually for performance — your duty is to not let
    untransformable poor performers remain.

- **Good vs Bad Reasons for Leaving ("zingers")**: A lens for whether a
  departure was preventable.
  - When to use: Reflecting after someone hands in notice.
  - How: Good reasons (new opportunity, family, life-changing compensation)
    show no malice and aren't your fault — facilitate the exit. Bad reasons
    ("zingers") are surprises you could have prevented (pay unhappiness,
    coworker clash, hidden promotion desire, boredom) rooted in a lack of
    open, candid communication.

- **The Fight-or-Release Decision**: How to respond when someone hands in
  notice.
  - When to use: The moment notice lands and the ball is in your court.
  - How: Fight only if they're a top performer AND you can actually satisfy
    the real catalyst (money, career progression, variety, frustrations)
    within the company. Never fight if you can't provide conditions that make
    them genuinely happier — you'd only defer the departure. Fighting means
    accepting some imbalance (higher pay, team change, others covering slack).

- **Performance Improvement Plan (PIP)**: A formal document that forces
  resolution of an ongoing, already-discussed performance problem.
  - When to use: Only after continual direct conversations about poor
    performance have gone nowhere — never as a surprise.
  - How: The document states (1) the current situation and evidenced issues,
    (2) measurable, achievable goals proving good performance, (3) a fixed
    active period (typically one to three months), and (4) how you'll help
    them succeed. Run weekly check-ins in one-to-ones, escalate to a formal
    warning on breach, then dismissal on further breach. Clear with HR and
    your manager first; check local labor law.

## Key Concepts

- **Attrition / turnover**: Natural loss of staff — the US tech sector runs
  ~13.2% yearly, the highest of any sector, so replacement is constant.
- **Zinger**: A departure that blindsides you but was preventable in
  retrospect, caused by simmering issues never surfaced.
- **Counteroffer**: A formulated change in compensation, team, hours, or work
  type, delivered in person and by email, with days to consider.
- **Good leaver**: Someone who exits amicably, gets a reference, hands over
  cleanly, and is welcomed back if the new role fails.
- **Fair PIP**: A plan with achievable goals genuinely aimed at recovery —
  unachievable PIPs are unethical and potentially unlawful.
- **Formal warning**: The escalation step after a PIP breach — evidenced,
  HR-approved, delivered in a meeting as the last chance.
- **Layoff / redundancy**: A special involuntary case where the employee did
  nothing wrong; a business decision, a surprise, delivered on a company
  script.

## Mental Models

- Think of turnover as sometimes beneficial: people who fit the company at one
  stage may not fit the next, and natural churn creates room to hire for
  current challenges.
- Use "your bar is fixed at your worst performer" — the longer poor
  performance sits unaddressed, the lower your standard as a manager appears.
- Think of a PIP as a probation period, not a firing in advance: a genuine
  win-win where either performance recovers or you get to hire a replacement.
- When someone gives notice, assume they've been mentally elsewhere for months
  — you're already on the back foot, so decide fast whether the catalyst is
  fixable.

## Anti-patterns

- **Reacting with anger or betrayal to notice**: Most departures carry no
  malice; treating them as ingratitude damages the relationship and your
  reputation.
- **Fighting to keep everyone**: Keeping staff introduces imbalance; fight
  only for top performers whose catalyst you can actually satisfy.
- **Counteroffering when you can't fix the real cause**: You only defer the
  departure and waste goodwill.
- **Dropping a PIP as a surprise**: A PIP with no prior performance
  conversations is a betrayal of trust and often unfair.
- **Setting unachievable PIP goals**: A firing-in-advance PIP is unethical,
  potentially unlawful, and corrosive to team trust.
- **Leaking confidential layoff information**: The rumor mill never improves
  the situation and increases conflict.
- **Delegating the firing to your manager**: You owe your staff your presence
  through the bad times — do the firing yourself even when nervous.

## Worked Example

The chapter walks a full PIP for a hypothetical senior engineer, Stephen,
whose performance has declined. The introduction states plainly that he is
being placed on a PIP, then lists specific, evidenced concerns: a four-day
unexplained absence in January without notice; missing at least three weekly
team meetings by arriving late despite a remote dial-in option; being
unresponsive to essential communication for over 24 hours while working from
home; and missing sprint commitments over 50% of the time. It notes teammates
raised this repeatedly because it blocked their own work. The objectives
section then sets measurable, time-boxed goals (all three months): complete
estimated work on time and flag early if not; agree a responsive line of
communication for remote days; be on time for all weekly meetings; inform the
team of booked vacation with handover; and notify the manager before 10:00
a.m. on any sick or off day. It fixes a three-month window ending September 4,
commits to weekly reviews and solicited peer feedback, and states the
consequence of failure (extension or contract termination) while offering
help. The document ends with blank spaces for agreed training/support and any
clarifications, plus both signatures.

## Key Takeaways

1. Internalize that turnover is normal (~13.2% yearly in tech) and sometimes
   beneficial — set expectations so a resignation doesn't devastate you.
2. Facilitate good departures: agree an end date together, offer a reference,
   do a thorough handover, start hiring, and let them go with a standing offer
   to return.
3. Prevent "zingers" by building career progression, Maslow's hierarchy check,
   and pet-peeve conversations into regular one-to-ones so issues surface
   early.
4. On notice, decide fast whether to fight — only for top performers whose
   real catalyst (money, progression, variety, frustration) you can genuinely
   satisfy — and enlist your manager, HR, and peer managers.
5. Address poor performance directly and continually; a PIP is only for when
   those conversations have failed, never a surprise.
6. Write PIPs with specific evidence and achievable, measurable, time-boxed
   goals; clear them with HR and your manager, and run weekly check-ins tied
   to the document.
7. For layoffs and firings, stick to the script, stay centered against anger,
   do it yourself, lean on HR and your manager, and take space for yourself
   afterward.
