# Chapter 3: Interfacing with Humans

## Core Idea

Management is a new toolset built on three interfaces to people:
communicating candidly, delegating work through others, and actively
working the relationship with your own manager to multiply your output.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Three Mediums of Communication**: every workplace exchange is
  spoken, written, or nonverbal (gestures, body language, tone) — all
  three deserve deliberate improvement.
  - When to use: any time you communicate; audit your own habits across
    all three.
  - How: match the medium to the message; be aware nonverbal signals
    leak your mood even when your words hide it; unify your voice across
    all three.

- **Choosing the Right Medium**: think about the message first, then the
  recipient, then adapt to the platform.
  - When to use: before sending any consequential communication.
  - How: face-to-face for real connection and hard messages (reviews,
    difficult news); email for formal, archival, asynchronous content;
    documents for formalizing ideas like architecture; chat for quick,
    synchronous, informal exchange; ticket/PM tools for workflow only.

- **Radical Candor** (from Kim Scott's book of the same name): the
  quadrant formed by two axes — care personally and challenge directly.
  - When to use: every time you give feedback.
  - How: care personally so feedback lands as being in their interest;
    challenge directly so you say exactly what you think. The four
    quadrants: Radical Candor (care + challenge), Ruinous Empathy (care,
    no challenge), Obnoxious Aggression (challenge, no care),
    Manipulative Insincerity (neither).

- **Accountability vs Responsibility (Delegation)**: delegation is
  giving responsibility for a task to someone else while retaining
  accountability for it.
  - When to use: whenever you hand off work.
  - How: keep accountability (you answer for the outcome); transfer
    responsibility (they do the work). Giving away both is abdication;
    keeping both yourself is the overwhelmed non-delegator.

- **The Scale of Delegation**: a left-to-right scale from no delegation
  to full delegation; each task is placed individually.
  - When to use: for each task, judged on the person's expertise for
    that specific task and how much control the task's criticality
    demands.
  - How: full delegation only when the person can meet your standard
    unaided; between the extremes apply teaching, coaching, mentorship,
    and check-ins. More delegation means less control — accept that
    except for critical work. Rule: given the control the task needs,
    delegate as much as you can.

- **The Four Ps (weekly summary)**: a prompt structure for the weekly
  written summary to your manager.
  - When to use: when you don't know what to write in your weekly
    reflection.
  - How: Progress (what happened), Problems (what needs addressing),
    Plans (how you'll approach them), People (how individuals are
    doing).

## Key Concepts

- **Verbal communication**: spoken and written combined; the author
  splits them because each has distinct considerations.
- **Measure twice, cut once**: think before broadcasting because a
  manager's word carries the weight of the org chart and can't be
  uncut.
- **Suitability trumps efficiency**: choose the delivery that is best
  for the recipient, not the one that saves you work.
- **Abdication**: handing over both responsibility and accountability —
  fire-and-forget delegation, which is not delegation at all.
- **Micromanaging**: retaining accountability over the process rather
  than the outputs; you should own outputs, not method.
- **Pull, don't push**: proactively drive the relationship with your
  manager rather than waiting to be directed.
- **Weekly written summary**: a thirty-minute reflective note to your
  manager that opens your world and doubles as managerial "rubber
  ducking."
- **Leverage (your output)**: your output equals your team's output
  plus the output of others you influence.

## Mental Models

- Think of delegation as an API for getting work done through others —
  a stable interface where you own the contract (accountability) but not
  the implementation (their process).
- Use "it's not about you" when composing any message: reread it as the
  recipient, first-time, and check tone and fit before sending.
- Think of a critical incident as sliding your delegation leftward:
  check in more on a senior engineer fixing production than on the same
  fix in dev.
- Use the weekly summary as rubber ducking — writing problems down makes
  your thinking clearer and often surfaces the solution before your
  manager even replies.

## Anti-patterns

- **Not delegating at all**: the overwhelmed manager doing critical work
  themselves while the team sits idle; poorer quality, higher stress,
  and staff feel untrusted.
- **Fire and forget (abdication)**: handing off responsibility and
  accountability; work goes unmonitored and often lands late, wrong, or
  undone.
- **Taking a task back**: snatching delegated work and doing it yourself
  when frustrated; cheats staff of learning and makes them feel bad —
  move the scale left instead.
- **Ruinous empathy**: caring but not challenging; unspecific praise or
  diluted criticism that never helps anyone improve.
- **Overcommunication, waffling, playing to the crowd, inconsistency,
  letting emotion get in the way**: the five communication traits to
  avoid.
- **Communicating when you want to, not when you need to**: spreading
  unconfirmed rumors to raise your own social capital.

## Worked Example

A CEO of a large multinational cannot be a hands-on expert in finance,
operations, sales, product, technology, and marketing. So the CEO stays
accountable for everything the company does but delegates responsibility
down the chain: technology goes to the CTO, who delegates to VPs, who
delegate to engineering managers, who delegate to engineers. If an
engineer maliciously leaks customer data, the engineer is not the one
answering to the press — the CEO is accountable and must take corrective
action. This shows delegation cascading through the org while
accountability remains attached at every level: responsibility flows
down, accountability is retained.

## Key Takeaways

1. Match the medium to the message and the recipient — face-to-face for
   hard or human conversations, email for archival, chat for quick and
   informal.
2. Check your own energy between activities; don't carry frustration
   from one meeting into the next, and say so up front if you do.
3. Give feedback with radical candor: care personally and challenge
   directly, every time.
4. Delegate responsibility but never accountability; place each task
   individually on the delegation scale and delegate as much as the
   needed control allows.
5. Own outputs, not process — expecting others to work exactly as you do
   is micromanaging.
6. Pull on your manager: book the meeting, ask the questions, and send a
   weekly written summary structured around the Four Ps.
