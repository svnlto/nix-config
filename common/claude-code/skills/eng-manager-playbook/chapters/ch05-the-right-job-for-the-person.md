# Chapter 5: The Right Job for the Person

## Core Idea

The manager's job is not to find the right person for the job but to
craft the right job for each person — matching work, challenge, and
environment to what actually motivates and grows that individual.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs (mapped to work)**: Five stacked levels
  of human need where each level unlocks the one above it.
  - When to use: Diagnosing why a report is disengaged despite good pay,
    or planning how to raise someone's fulfillment at work.
  - How: Map the five levels to work — Physiological (fair pay,
    benefits), Safety (job security, safe environment), Love/belonging
    (good colleagues and manager relationships), Esteem (recognition,
    title, promotions), Self-actualization (challenge, growth, career
    advancement). Company provides the bottom two; you as manager
    directly influence the top three.

- **Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)**: The band of tasks a person
  cannot do alone yet but can complete with help from someone more
  skilled; finishing them expands the zone toward harder work.
  - When to use: Deciding what to delegate, and to whom, so that work
    doubles as growth.
  - How: Give a person tasks just beyond their solo reach, pair them
    with a more knowledgeable colleague, let them complete it with
    assistance, then raise the difficulty. Apply at task level
    (delegation plus mentorship) and at career level (skill trees).

- **Skill Trees (career-level ZPD)**: Borrowed from role-playing games —
  break a large career goal into an ordered tree of prerequisite
  milestones, each a stretch within the current ZPD.
  - When to use: A report names a big, distant goal (speak at a
    conference, become a CTO) that feels like a pipe dream.
  - How: Place the end goal at the root, split it into subgoals, then
    lay out ordered, tangible milestones. Start with the first
    achievable milestone, work it within the ZPD, celebrate on arrival,
    then plan the next.

- **The Cathedral and the Bazaar (as a motivation lens)**: Reinterprets
  Raymond's two open-source development models as two personality types.
  - When to use: Understanding why one report craves stability and
    another craves change, and staffing them accordingly.
  - How: Identify each report as a cathedral constructor (motivated by
    control, focus, depth, mastery) or a bazaar browser (motivated by
    chaos, variety, newness, speed). Build that trait into how you
    delegate, what opportunities you offer, and which environment you
    place them in.

## Key Concepts

- **Self-actualization**: The top of Maslow's pyramid — realizing full
  potential through mastering skills and achieving goals; the need most
  under a manager's influence.
- **Basic needs**: The bottom two Maslow layers (physiological, safety);
  money and possessions largely live here, so more money past
  sufficiency does not raise happiness.
- **Zone of proximal development**: The learning sweet spot between what
  a person can do alone and what they cannot yet do at all.
- **Task-level proximal development**: Delegating a stretch task and
  pairing a junior with a senior so both grow — one in skill, one in
  mentoring.
- **Career-level proximal development**: Using skill trees to push a
  person's development frontier toward ambitious long-term goals.
- **Cathedral constructor**: A person who thrives on focus, stability,
  purism, deep mastery, and being the company expert on one thing.
- **Bazaar browser**: A person who thrives on variety, change, chaos,
  prototyping, and sampling as many new things as possible.

## Mental Models

- Think of every task as a choice: either "something that needs doing"
  or "a chance to advance someone's zone of proximal development."
- Use a skill tree when a goal feels too distant to act on — begin with
  the end in mind, then work backward to the first achievable milestone.
- Think of cathedral versus bazaar as preference, not virtue — neither
  is right or wrong, and a person's camp can shift with life
  circumstances.
- Use Maslow's map to see that a highly paid but boring job only meets
  basic needs, which is why people quit it or take pay cuts for autonomy.

## Anti-patterns

- **Chasing "the right person for the job"**: No single hire can do
  everything in an ever-changing field; the durable move is shaping the
  job around the person you have.
- **Treating ping-pong tables and pay as higher-level needs**: Perks and
  salary sit at the base of the pyramid and do not deliver
  self-actualization.
- **Giving a bazaar browser long stints on one stable area**: Reads as
  stagnation to them and quietly drives underperformance.
- **Trapping a cathedral constructor in constant churn**: Denies the
  deep focus and mastery they need to be in their happy place.
- **Assuming you already know each report's motivation**: Preconceptions
  are often wrong — the disengaged person may just need more variety, or
  the challenge you gave may be uncomfortable rather than exciting.

## Worked Example

A report says they want to speak at an international conference but
admits speaking is a weakness and they lack a suitable project. Rather
than dismiss it, the manager builds a skill tree. The goal splits into
two subgoals: attaining speaking skills, and working on a
conference-worthy project. Tackling the speaking subgoal first, the
manager asks how the report currently rates their speaking, where and
how often they have spoken, and how it went. It emerges they have given
only a handful of talks and feel rusty. Together they draw a tree whose
first milestone is "speak at a local meetup." One fork: pick an
accomplished internal speaker (the manager introduces them) and an
admired external speaker (watch their talks on YouTube), then report
back what makes each effective. Second fork: take charge of the monthly
new-hire onboarding session — familiar content, fresh audience each
month — plus a speaking-skills course. Once both forks are done, deliver
a technical talk to progressively harder audiences: first the internal
colleague, then the department's tech-talk slot, then a local meetup of
strangers. On reaching the milestone they celebrate and plan the next
one. The tree does not reach the conference in one leap, and the
technical progression lives in a separate tree — but each step is a
tangible stretch inside the ZPD.

## Key Takeaways

1. Map each report onto Maslow's work hierarchy and invest your energy
   where you have leverage: belonging, esteem, and self-actualization.
2. Delegate stretch tasks inside each person's ZPD and pair juniors with
   seniors so the same task grows two people at once.
3. Turn distant career goals into skill trees — ordered, achievable
   milestones — so progress is measurable and motivating.
4. Have the explicit conversation about whether each report is a
   cathedral constructor or a bazaar browser, then staff to it.
5. Check your preconceptions in one-to-ones; the trait that drives
   someone is often the opposite of what you assumed.
6. Some person-team fits are impossible even with time — accept that a
   move to another team may be the right answer.
