# Chapter 15: Dual Ladders

## Core Idea

Individual contribution and management are two separate jobs on two
separate, equally rewarded career ladders; a company that fails to
define both forces good engineers to leave or to become reluctant
managers just to get promoted.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Dual ladders (IC track and management track)**: two parallel
  promotion paths so people can grow in seniority without switching jobs.
  - When to use: whenever your company lacks a defined path, or ties
    promotion solely to becoming a manager.
  - How: define the IC levels (L1 junior, L2 engineer, L3 senior, L4-L5
    staff, L6+ principal) and the management levels (L3 engineering
    manager, L4 director, L5-L6 VP engineering, L7+ CTO); line the levels
    up by experience; ensure no glass ceiling and comparable pay at
    comparable contribution.

- **The career progression grid**: a matrix with roles across the top
  and competencies down the side, filled in to describe what each level
  looks like.
  - When to use: to give staff a shared vocabulary for where they are and
    where they want to go.
  - How: pick roles (e.g. junior, engineer, senior); pick competencies
    (industry experience, technical knowledge, mentorship, conflict
    resolution, communication, influence, efficiency, company values);
    run a team workshop to describe each cell; treat cells as a compass,
    not GPS or a checkbox list.

- **30-60-90-day plan with a get-out clause**: a safety net for engineers
  trying management for the first time.
  - When to use: when a strong IC is nervous about the leap into
    management.
  - How: with their new manager, set objectives for each 30/60/90-day
    window (formal handover, start weekly one-to-ones, deploy a fix,
    etc.); write in an explicit clause that they may return to their old
    role at any time with no hard feelings.

- **Compensation algorithm (zero-sum raise budget)**: a rule-of-thumb
  order for distributing a fixed raise pool.
  - When to use: when allocating a fixed budget across a group.
  - How: 1) fix anyone underpaid vs market or peers; 2) apply promotion
    increases to market rate; 3) apply inflation/cost-of-living to
    everyone else; 4) spend the remainder on performance, giving
    less-experienced fast-growers higher-percentage raises to offset the
    fact that a flat percentage grows big salaries more.

## Key Concepts

- **Individual contributor (IC)**: someone whose currency is technical
  expertise and influence and who line-manages nobody.
- **Level**: a stage in a track, written "L" plus a number, lined up
  across both ladders by experience.
- **Impact**: the observable measure of IC seniority — code quality, net
  independent contribution, collaboration, mentorship, influence,
  innovation, and effect on the bottom line.
- **Competency**: a skill dimension (e.g. influence, mentorship) that is
  described differently at each level of the grid.
- **Compass not GPS**: a framework gives a general direction to find your
  own way, not a fixed route to follow whether you like it or not.
- **The Peter Principle**: Laurence J. Peter's observation that people are
  promoted on past success until they reach a level where they are no
  longer competent.
- **Manager span of control**: rule of thumb of at most ~7 direct reports
  before one-to-one time becomes prohibitive.

## Mental Models

- Think of a manager's output as the output of their team plus the
  output of everyone they influence; the surest way to grow it is to grow
  the team.
- Think of a promotion as ratifying a position already almost reached —
  the first night on stage after years of practice, not being thrown into
  the spotlight with no cue. No promotion should surprise the person's
  peers.
- Use "build people, not software" to translate IC competencies onto the
  management track — same competencies, different raw material.
- Treat the track you are on as your primary function: coding as a
  manager is fine until it starts costing your managerial duties.

## Anti-patterns

- **Making management the only way up**: pushes engineers who love
  building into a job they may not want, and drives leavers.
- **Checkbox promotions**: discrete criteria to "unlock" a level are
  impossible to measure objectively and invite gaming and unethical
  behavior.
- **Surprise promotions**: if a senior promotion is not self-evident to
  peers, you probably made the wrong call.
- **Glass ceilings between tracks**: paying senior managers more than
  principal engineers (or vice versa) at equal contribution reveals
  leadership bias and caps one path.
- **Coding to the detriment of the team**: a manager who keeps shipping
  code while managerial responsibilities slip needs to rethink their time.

## Worked Example

A senior engineer on your team applies to run another team internally.
You know they would excel, but they are scared of being trapped if they
dislike management. Instead of letting that fear block the move, you help
them build a 30-60-90-day plan with their new manager: by day 30, complete
a formal handover and start weekly one-to-ones; by day 60, ship a fix to
production and run their first iteration planning; by day 90, be running
the team's rituals independently. Crucially, you bake in a get-out clause
stating that if it is not working out at any point, they can return to
their old IC role with no hard feelings. This prevents the Peter Principle
from turning a high performer into an unhappy, less-productive manager, and
prevents the company from accidentally managing them out the door.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define both ladders explicitly and line their levels up by experience;
   IC and management are different jobs, not a hierarchy.
2. Build the grid as a team workshop — roles across the top, competencies
   down the side — and treat it as a compass, never a checkbox list.
3. Give first-time managers a 30-60-90-day plan with a get-out clause so
   trying management is reversible.
4. Never surprise anyone with a promotion; back it with accumulating peer
   feedback so it reads as ratification.
5. Reuse IC competencies on the management track, reframed as building
   people rather than software.
6. When splitting a fixed raise budget, fix underpayment first, then
   promotions, then cost-of-living, then performance.
7. Publishing or improving a progression framework is high-impact
   influence work that reaches beyond your own team.
