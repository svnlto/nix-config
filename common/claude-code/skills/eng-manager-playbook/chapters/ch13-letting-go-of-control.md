# Chapter 13: Letting Go of Control

## Core Idea

You do your work through other people, so clinging to control eats you
alive and makes your output worse; letting go — of tasks, of compulsive
input, and of work after work — paradoxically makes you a more effective
manager and a healthier human.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Trichotomy of Control** (Irvine, extending Epictetus'
  dichotomy): sort every worry into three buckets to protect your
  tranquility.
  - When to use: any time you catch yourself anxious about a delegated
    task, a project outcome, or a person's decision.
  - How: (1) Things you fully control (your desires, effort, goals). (2)
    Things you truly cannot control (the weather, the past). (3) Things
    you partly control (winning a match, a project's success). For the
    third bucket, set internal goals (do your best in the situation you
    are in), never external ones (hit 15.4% growth) — external goals hang
    your happiness on what you cannot command.

- **L-mode / R-mode** (from Pragmatic Thinking and Learning): model the
  brain as a dual-CPU, single-bus computer where only one mode runs at a
  time.
  - When to use: when you feel busy but uncreative, drowning in
    reactive tasks with no new insight.
  - How: L-mode is linear, sequential execution (answering email,
    writing code line by line). R-mode is holistic pattern-matching that
    surfaces novel connections but cannot be forced. Deliberately
    disengage L-mode to give R-mode room. Maps to Kahneman's System 2
    (L-mode) and System 1 (R-mode).

- **The 85% capacity rule**: plan your week to fill only about 85% of
  your capacity so you can absorb the inevitable emergencies.
  - When to use: sprint planning and weekly commitment-setting.
  - How: take on 85% of what you think you can finish; block 30-minute
    react/think slots at each end of the day; audit a month of activities
    and cut low-value ones; accept the guilt of unfilled time.

## Key Concepts

- **Tranquility**: the Stoic term for peace of mind — the state you
  protect by not betting your emotions on outcomes you cannot control.
- **Internal vs external goals**: internal goals judge your effort in
  the moment; external goals judge results you do not command.
- **Negative visualization**: a Stoic practice of imagining loss to both
  prepare for it and better appreciate what you have now.
- **False productivity**: the reactive dopamine loop of clearing
  notifications and inboxes that feels like work but crowds out real
  impact.
- **10% R-mode time**: roughly a tenth of each week blocked to do
  nothing but let thoughts emerge, away from the desk.
- **Burn-out** (WHO, 2019): a syndrome from chronic unmanaged workplace
  stress — energy depletion, cynicism about the job, and reduced
  efficacy.
- **Batching communication**: opening email and chat only at a few
  mindful times a day and closing them otherwise.

## Mental Models

- Use the trichotomy of control when you cannot sleep for worry: if the
  outcome is out of your hands, your only honest job is to have tried
  your best.
- Think of R-mode as the shower where the solution arrives — you cannot
  summon it, you can only build the quiet conditions that let it fire.
- Treat sleep as the best legal performance-enhancing drug: it is a
  one-time offer you cannot claw back on the weekend.
- Run at 85% so being available is a feature, not an accident — the
  manager at 100% becomes the weak link who drops the urgent request.

## Anti-patterns

- **Snatching delegated work back under pressure**: it robs the person
  of mastery and autonomy, and re-centralizes the very output you are
  meant to multiply through others.
- **Judging yourself by external goals**: you set yourself up for
  permanent unhappiness because the result was never fully yours.
- **Compulsive notification checking**: engineered dopamine hits keep
  you reactive and starve the holistic, creative work that actually
  moves the needle.
- **The hustle-at-4 a.m. myth**: sold by people monetizing your
  attention; long hours and gadget-lit bedtimes degrade the sleep,
  exercise, and presence that make you good.

## Worked Example

A member of staff hints they may leave. The reflexive worry — "I must
make them stay" — is an external goal: whether they go is ultimately not
in your control. Applying the trichotomy, you reframe to an internal
goal: do your best to counteroffer and accommodate them to the limit of
your ability. You prepare the strongest fair offer, have the honest
conversation, and remove what obstacles you can. If they still leave,
your tranquility survives intact because you did the part that was
actually yours. The author walks the same move through a product launch
(don't bet on its success, do make it as good as possible) and a
slipping deadline (don't panic about lateness, do pull the levers of
scope, resources, and time).

## Key Takeaways

1. For anything you only partly control, set an internal goal — "I did
   my best here" — and refuse to stake your mood on the external result.
2. Block roughly 10% of your week, away from the desk with no agenda, to
   let R-mode surface insights, and carry a capture tool to note them.
3. Plan to 85% capacity so you have slack to absorb outages, upset
   teammates, and end-of-year work without becoming a bottleneck.
4. Batch email and chat to a few set times a day; close them the rest of
   the time and resist the urge to peek.
5. Protect eight hours of sleep: cut late caffeine and alcohol, keep
   gadgets out of the bedroom, keep it cool, and keep a fixed schedule.
6. Build movement into the day — walk or cycle in, move five minutes an
   hour, hold walking one-to-ones — and practice a few minutes of
   mindful breathing.
