# Chapter 9: How to Win Friends and Influence People

## Core Idea

Your output as a manager includes the output of others you influence, so
building a wide company network and giving back through mentoring and
coaching multiplies your impact far beyond your own team.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Two modes of conversation (Directive vs. Following interest)**: every
  coaching exchange swings between a "push" mode where you solve the
  problem for them and a "pull" mode where you help them solve it
  themselves.
  - When to use: any coaching conversation, formal or in the kitchen
  - How: use directive ("push") to unblock stuck thinking or teach a
    skill; use following interest ("pull") — listening, reflecting,
    summarizing — to make them reach the conclusion; less experienced
    staff need more directive, experienced staff need more pull
- **Keep the thought bubble over their head**: when you feel the urge to
  jump in with the answer, imagine pushing a cartoon thought bubble from
  your head onto theirs and ask an open, leading question instead.
  - When to use: whenever you already know the solution but want the
    person to develop the skill of finding it
  - How: ask "What's hard about it?", "Have we solved this before?",
    "Do you know what they used?"; only if truly stuck, be directive in
    a way that still lets them act ("I've a feeling Alice's team did
    something similar — go chat with her")
- **GROW model**: a four-stage structure that keeps a coaching session on
  course.
  - When to use: structured one-off or recurring coaching sessions on a
    specific problem
  - How: Goal (what problem are we solving?), Reality (situation now —
    who/what/where/how much?), Options (all the different ways to tackle
    it), Wrap-up (commit to a choice and name the support needed)
- **Mentorship agreement (five questions)**: mentor and mentee answer
  framing questions before their first meeting, mirroring the one-to-one
  contracting exercise.
  - When to use: kicking off any new mentoring relationship
  - How: agree on Time (start/end/cadence/place), Flexibility (anytime
    contact vs. batched), Confidentiality, Boundaries (what the
    relationship does and does not cover), Review and evaluation, and
    "What if it doesn't work out?" (escalation path to the mentee's
    manager)

## Key Concepts

- **The output equation**: your output = the output of your team + the
  output of others that you influence.
- **Nudging**: exerting positive influence by offering an opinion that
  shifts another team's decision toward a better outcome.
- **Being a role model**: publicly acting with the values and behaviors
  you want others to adopt — a second form of positive influence.
- **The snow melts at the periphery**: staff on the business edges
  (sales, customer success) see market reality before the core does, so
  connecting with them speeds the business's reaction.
- **Mentoring**: a long-lived, mostly directive teacher/student
  relationship where one person shares experience, skills, and knowledge
  to help another progress.
- **Coaching**: a framework of conversational tools that helps anyone
  improve performance and solve their own problems, even in domains you
  know nothing about.
- **Mentorship matrix**: a shared spreadsheet (name, title, team,
  contact, skills sought, skills offered) that kick-starts a mentoring
  culture across a department.
- **Virtuous cycle of influence**: influencing others positively builds
  connections, which gather more information, which feeds better
  decisions — the four managerial activities reinforcing each other.

## Mental Models

- Think of influence not as politics but as opportunity: more
  connections simply means more people you can positively affect, which
  raises your own output.
- Use coaching when you cannot rely on shared expertise — with staff
  more senior than you, or in departments whose work you do not
  understand, listening and questioning still helps them progress.
- Think of a neutral third-party coaching or mentoring role as
  connection without judgment: you build trust because you are not
  accountable for the person's pay or performance.
- Use an out-of-the-blue introduction email freely — a polite colleague
  introducing themselves almost never triggers a negative reaction.

## Anti-patterns

- **Telling an expert or senior person the answer**: it robs them of the
  chance to build reusable problem-solving skills; push the thought
  bubble instead.
- **Waiting for your manager to make all your connections**: being well
  connected takes concerted personal effort, especially for introverts.
- **Thrusting mentorship on people**: a mentorship should be initiated
  by the mentee; create conditions for it (e.g. a matrix) rather than
  imposing it.
- **Entering a mentorship with no boundaries**: without an agreement the
  mentor gets pulled into owning the mentee's deadlines and the
  relationship blurs.

## Worked Example

A report says the back end is much harder than expected and they do not
know how to approach it. Rather than solving it, the manager keeps the
thought bubble overhead. "OK, so what's hard about it?" They explain
MySQL will be too slow because every access scans every row. The manager
still does not solve it: "Interesting. Have we solved any storage
problems like this before?" The report recalls Alice's team hit a
similar wall storing alerts. "That project was quite successful. Do you
know what they used?" "I don't — but I'll grab Alice after this meeting."
Framed with GROW: Goal — store data fast enough for the use case;
Reality — we know relational DBs but they are too slow, and there is a
hard deadline; Options — hire an expensive consultant, experiment
ourselves without production expertise, or ask whoever solved it before;
Wrap-up — seek Alice's team's advice, meet later, then decide. The
manager taught a reusable thinking approach without ever giving the
answer.

## Key Takeaways

1. Build a deliberately diverse network — across departments, locations,
   genders, and seniorities — so your input is better informed and
   balanced.
2. Ask peers and your manager who they interact with and why, then
   introduce yourself by email; you cannot expect connections to be made
   for you.
3. Mix formal quarterly email check-ins with informal monthly coffees;
   the softer, agenda-free conversations often surface the most.
4. Default to coaching over telling — keep the thought bubble over their
   head and follow their interest until they hone in on the answer.
5. Contract every mentorship up front with the five questions so
   boundaries and expectations are explicit before the first meeting.
6. Get a mentor and a coach for yourself too; senior managers may need
   an external coach for problems too sensitive to share internally.
