# Chapter 7: Join Us

## Core Idea

Hiring is a manager's highest-leverage act for increasing team output, so
optimize for the whole team's balance and each candidate's potential to grow —
not for cloning yourself or stacking the most senior people you can find — and
treat every candidate with kindness at every stage.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Hiring Iron Triangle (good/fast/cheap, reworked)**: The classic
  triple constraint applied to hiring — you get two of three, never all.
  - When to use: Setting expectations before you open a requisition, so you
    stop waiting for a perfect hire that will never arrive.
  - How: Accept one of these realities — a fast senior hire will bust budget
    (headhunting, recruiter fees); a fast in-budget hire won't have all the
    experience you want; a perfect in-budget senior hire won't arrive on your
    timeline. Hire for potential within budget and a reasonable timescale.

- **Job Description Template (five-part journey)**: A reusable structure that
  takes the applicant on a journey rather than listing requirements.
  - When to use: Writing any job advert.
  - How: (1) Describe the company — why it exists, its purpose, non-technical
    and inspiring; (2) Describe the role — why it matters, what it entails,
    with concrete example projects; (3) Who you're looking for — broad traits,
    not a skills laundry list; (4) Salary and benefits — always list the band;
    (5) How to apply — clear, unambiguous instructions.

- **The Interview Funnel (six stages)**: A pipeline where the probability of
  an offer rises as candidates progress, and time invested rises with it.
  - When to use: Designing or running any interview process.
  - How: (1) Review applications; (2) Screening calls; (3) First interview
    (hiring manager + one teammate); (4) Optional technical exercise
    (take-home); (5) Final interview (two more colleagues); (6) Make the offer.
    Spend least time on those least likely to be hired, most on the rest.

- **Three-Factor Offer Calibration**: What an offer must satisfy at once.
  - When to use: Deciding a number before extending an offer.
  - How: Balance (1) the salaries of their future peers; (2) what other local
    companies pay for the role; (3) the candidate's experience and impact.
    Justify the number; never lowball or barter.

## Key Concepts

- **Output of a manager**: The output of your team plus the output of others
  you influence — the metric hiring should maximize.
- **Interdependent team**: A cross-functional unit (engineers, designers, QA,
  agile practitioner, product manager, EM) whose members depend on each other,
  so balance matters more than raw individual talent.
- **Too much talent effect**: In highly interdependent teams, talent boosts
  performance only to a point, after which coordination suffers and output
  falls.
- **Culture add (vs culture fit)**: You cannot "fit" a culture; good hires
  contribute to and extend it, so hire for what a person adds, not sameness.
- **Screening call**: A 15-30 minute video call to form an initial connection
  and resolve resume questions (relocation, visa, notice, salary
  expectations).
- **Take-home test**: An optional, short (couple-hours), clearly-scoped
  exercise mirroring real work, useful for nervous candidates and graduates.
- **Gender-coded language**: Masculine- or feminine-themed wording that
  subconsciously excludes applicants, checkable with a gender-decoder tool.
- **Regrettable vs non-regrettable rejection**: Every rejected candidate
  should still leave with a positive impression and be encouraged to reapply.

## Mental Models

- Think of hiring as an opportunity to pause and assess the whole
  interdependent team, not just to fill a seat — ask where the team bottlenecks
  before defaulting to "hire another engineer."
- Use potential over pedigree: assess how much a candidate can grow into the
  role, not just the skills they arrive with.
- Treat accepting an offer as a two-way choice — the candidate is also
  interviewing you, so be your genuine self so they see their real future
  manager.
- Run the interview like a one-to-one with a teammate you're mentoring: work
  problems together, not an examination you administer.

## Anti-patterns

- **Just hire more engineers**: Throwing engineers at every problem ignores
  where the team actually bottlenecks (often QA or organization).
- **Hire the most senior person possible**: Over-stacking seniors breeds
  conflict over direction and removes mentoring opportunities; it can lower
  team output.
- **Hiring people just like yourself**: "Culture fit" as sameness (same
  background, humor, hobbies, drinking buddies) narrows the team and encodes
  bias.
- **Skills laundry list in the advert**: A long requirements list repels
  applicants — women on average apply only at 100% match, men at ~60%.
- **Brain-teaser interviews**: Streetlight-counting and blender puzzles reveal
  nothing about diligent day-to-day work and stress candidates out.
- **Lowballing the offer**: Starting low to barter up is insulting and signals
  bad faith after the candidate invested real time.
- **Panel intimidation**: More than two interviewers, or confrontational
  seating, makes candidates perform worse than their true ability.

## Worked Example

The book walks a full Dyncompsys ("making every home a smart home") job
description end to end. The company paragraph is non-technical and
vision-first. The role section sells a senior Android engineer opportunity by
naming concrete projects (controls for new smart-light-bulbs, automation
recipes, sharing moments with family) and explicitly says "nobody starts any
role as an expert" — Java plus curiosity is enough. The "who we're looking
for" section names traits (curious, eager to learn, diligent, kind) and frames
seniority as mentorship, adding "we care less about how long you've been
active and more about what you've been doing." Salary is stated outright:
$150,000-$250,000 depending on experience and impact, plus health, dental,
retirement match, and summer Friday afternoons. The apply section gives an
email, invites a short written application over an essay, and welcomes
part-time and diverse candidates. The chapter then shows a rejection letter to
"Steve" that is warm, gives a light reason ("looking for more experience"),
and encourages reapplying.

## Key Takeaways

1. Before opening a req, ask where the interdependent team slows down and what
   the team itself would add — the answer may be a QA or agile practitioner,
   not another engineer.
2. Write job descriptions as a five-part journey selling potential and
   purpose; always publish the salary band and run the text through a
   gender-decoder tool.
3. Design interview stages as a funnel that spends time proportional to hire
   likelihood, and guarantee every candidate a kind, well-communicated
   experience.
4. Over-describe each interview step before doing it, allow for nerves, take
   notes on answers (not judgments), and build a body of evidence to counter
   unconscious bias.
5. If you're on the fence about a candidate, side with no — your team pays the
   cost of an underperformer.
6. Keep take-home tests under a couple of hours, clearly scoped, and mirroring
   real work; make them optional when a strong resume or GitHub already proves
   the skill.
7. Make a fair, justified offer that balances peer pay, market rate, and the
   candidate's impact — never lowball, and let go gracefully if they decline.
