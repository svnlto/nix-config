# Chapter 17: Startups

## Core Idea

Startups are one of the fastest paths into management and up the org
chart, and the management skills in this book are not big-company
overhead — they are the counter-balance that keeps a fast-growing,
chaotic startup from breaking.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The startup venture-capital life cycle**: the stages a startup passes
  through, each opening different management opportunities.
  - When to use: deciding when and how to approach a startup for a
    management role, matched to its funding stage.
  - How: track four stages — (1) Co-founders find problem-solution fit
    and build an MVP; (2) Initial investment (angels, accelerators,
    seed funds) to test whether more money grows impact — this is where
    "manager number one" appears; (3) VC rounds (Series A, B, C) that
    fund many hires, teams, and manager-of-managers roles; (4) Beyond,
    where the company is now as big as any established firm.

- **Becoming manager number one (startup VP engineering)**: the first
  dedicated management hire in engineering, owning the whole department.
  - When to use: after a startup takes initial seed investment, when the
    CTO is heads-down building and nobody owns process, hiring, or
    delivery.
  - How: own the delivery process end-to-end; own engineer performance
    (line management, one-to-ones, reviews); own resourcing and
    prioritization with limited people; own hiring and the hiring
    process itself; keep the CTO focused on building by absorbing
    everything else; and still contribute code.

- **CTO vs. VP engineering split**: the CTO creates the product, the VP
  engineering creates the engineering organization.
  - When to use: clarifying who does what in an early startup so the two
    roles complement rather than collide.
  - How: CTO drives technology and scaling of the product; VP
    engineering turns "a small band of pirates into an efficient naval
    vessel" with room for the fleet to expand.

## Key Concepts

- **Unicorn**: a startup valued at over $1 billion.
- **Product-market fit**: the point where a product reliably meets real
  market demand; most failed startups never reach it.
- **Problem-solution fit / MVP**: proving a buildable product solves a
  business problem, tested via a minimum viable product.
- **Manager number one**: the first person in engineering focused on
  management of people, process, hiring, communication, and delivery.
- **Vesting / stock options**: equity granted over time as
  compensation, often in lieu of a high base salary; may never convert
  to real money.
- **Tours of duty**: framing a role as a defined, mutually beneficial
  stint rather than lifetime employment.
- **Holacracy / Teal**: alternative self-organizing governance
  structures (circles, self-management) used by some startups instead of
  a CEO-down tree.

## Mental Models

- Think of startup VP engineering as an engineering-manager role with
  "accountability, risk, and autonomy turned up to 11" — if no process
  exists, you invent it.
- Use startups as career accelerators when the ladder at a big company
  is blocked: rapid growth manufactures new management rungs, and each
  person has outsized influence over how they are created.
- Think of one year at a startup as roughly five years elsewhere,
  because the intensity and variety round you out fast.
- Ask "am I a cathedral builder or a bazaar browser?" to gauge whether
  you can tolerate constant pivots and change before joining.

## Anti-patterns

- **Being put off by the lofty "VP" title**: it hides a hands-on role
  that a strong engineering manager can grow into — treating it as
  out-of-reach costs you the opportunity.
- **Betting your happiness on the equity**: most startups fail and even
  winners take 10+ years; counting on the payout invites disaster.
- **Assuming management has no place in a small company**: without a
  management mindset the startup grows chaotic; good management is light
  touch, not bureaucracy.
- **Skipping legal advice on equity terms**: not understanding vesting,
  stock class, or amounts leaves you exposed.

## Worked Example

The chapter opens on a jealousy trigger: former colleague Sam left the
team two years ago for a small delivery-app startup that just hit a
$3-billion valuation, and she is now VP Engineering. The reader passed
on that same startup's offer for a bigger salary at a larger company.

The chapter reframes the envy into a concrete plan by funding stage. If
you meet founders at the co-founder stage through your network, register
interest early — "what you're building looks exciting; if you grow, I'd
love to join as an engineer with scope to grow into a manager." At the
initial-investment stage, watch TechCrunch, Hacker News, AngelList, and
Product Hunt for raise announcements, find who works there, reach out
directly on LinkedIn or Twitter, and ask whether they need manager
number one. At the VC-round stage, expect a standard recruitment
process, but attach a short cover letter stating your intent to go down
the management track even when only IC roles are advertised. The lesson:
Sam's leap was not luck — it was getting in early where a management
vacuum existed and leaning into ownership.

## Key Takeaways

1. Watch for "manager number one" openings — small teams (low tens of
   people) that just raised seed money and have no one owning
   engineering process.
2. Don't wait for advertised roles; use your network and state your
   management intent directly, even when only IC positions are listed.
3. Expect lower base pay and treat equity as a bonus, not a plan — be
   there for the experience.
4. Take startup roles to sample many functions (full-stack, product,
   roadmap) and see a whole business up close.
5. Bring a management mindset as the counter-balance to a CTO with the
   pedal to the metal — guard process, quality, and scalability while
   the company sprints.
6. Only join if you are passionate about what's being built; the
   ownership and volatility are multiplied by 100.
