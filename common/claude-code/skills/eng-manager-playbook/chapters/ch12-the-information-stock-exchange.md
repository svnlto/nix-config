# Chapter 12: The Information Stock Exchange

## Core Idea

Information is a manager's currency, so act as a gatekeeper (filter and
share responsibly) rather than a spy (extract without permission).
Classify what you hold, share just enough consistently, and engage in
workplace politics for the greater good.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Spy vs. Gatekeeper**: Two opposing stances toward information; a
  manager must be the gatekeeper.
  - When to use: Any time you obtain or control sensitive information.
  - How: As gatekeeper, decide what information which parties hear, when
    it is shared, and how it is framed. Never obtain information without
    the holder's permission, for another party's benefit, or by deceitful
    means (the spy behaviors to avoid).

- **The two guiding rules**: Ethical defaults that get you most of the
  way with any information decision.
  - When to use: Every information-handling judgment call.
  - How: Apply the Hippocratic Oath ("first, do no harm") and the Golden
    Rule ("treat others as you would like to be treated").

- **Three information categories**: A classification scheme for
  sensitive information.
  - When to use: Before deciding whether and how to share anything.
  - How: Completely confidential (not shared at all, e.g. redundancies);
    closed box (the existence is shareable but contents are not, e.g. pay
    reviews in progress); open box (not sensitive, broadcast widely, e.g.
    team rosters).

- **Consistently Just Enough**: The two overarching principles across
  all categories.
  - When to use: Every act of sharing.
  - How: Be consistent in how you treat information across different
    people, and always share just enough — no more than each person
    needs.

- **The share-with test (five criteria)**: A checklist before telling a
  person something sensitive.
  - When to use: Whenever you feel compelled to share.
  - How: Share only if it is relevant to their job, they have a reason to
    know, it benefits you both, you share only what they need, and they
    can be trusted to keep it secret. Otherwise question your motive.

- **Disagree and Commit**: A principle for moving past the lack of
  consensus.
  - When to use: Complex decisions where full agreement is impossible.
  - How: State that consensus wasn't reached, but once the decision is
    finalized everyone commits to following it — preventing the
    disagreement from carrying past the decision boundary.

## Key Concepts

- **Gatekeeping**: Filtering information for further distribution,
  deciding what, when, and how to broadcast.
- **Known knowns / known unknowns / unknown unknowns**: Rumsfeld's
  distillation of how seniority increases both access to sensitive
  information and the care required to handle it.
- **Closed box**: Information whose existence is public but whose details
  are withheld — the hardest and most-mishandled category.
- **Leave nobody behind**: The manager's duty to keep everyone seeing the
  same version of the same information.
- **Trends toward transparency**: The default should be to share (with
  care), not to keep secret; silence makes people assume the worst.
- **Workplace politics**: How people in groups make decisions; a neutral
  force with positive and negative uses, not inherently backstabbing.
- **The energy bar**: A manager's finite store of authority, depleted by
  controversial overrides and unpopular orders, replenished by moving
  congruently with the team.

## Mental Models

- Think of information as currency: the more you hold, the better your
  decisions — but counterfeit currency (gossip, rumor, lies) exists too.
- Use the closed-box image (a wrapped present under the tree): announce
  that it exists and is progressing, but don't disclose the contents.
- Think of going rogue as technical debt: it gets worse the longer it
  continues and harder to back out from.
- Use the energy-bar model when deciding whether to override a decision:
  spend the bar tactically and sparingly.
- In the absence of information, people assume the worst — so don't let
  information be absent when you can share just enough.

## Anti-patterns

- **Being a spy**: Reading a salary spreadsheet you were sent by mistake,
  or prying at the bar for secondary facts to piece together — obtaining
  information without permission.
- **Defaulting closed-box info to fully confidential**: Silence on a pay
  review reads as "no reviews this year" when the truth may be positive
  (competitive benchmarking). Broadcast the existence, withhold details.
- **Misuse of power**: Ordering people around without winning hearts and
  minds erodes respect and depletes your influence.
- **Level-jumping**: Going over someone's head to their manager leaves
  the person in the middle feeling undermined (skip-levels are the
  exception).
- **Going rogue**: Making big changes silently instead of building
  consensus — causes worse conflict later.
- **Personal gain**: Using influence for referral bonuses or to
  marginalize others — the worst form of politicking; it always catches
  up with you.

## Worked Example

Ben spins round and shares a rumor: the CTO leaked all customer details,
including search logs, to another company. He insists "more people need
to know" and pushes the manager to tell the team, but when pressed on how
he knows, he offers only "trust me." Moments later the CTO herself walks
by, seeming entirely normal, and the manager is left rattled and unsure
the thing even happened.

Handled through the gatekeeper lens, the manager should not broadcast.
The information fails the share-with test — its provenance is unverified
("where did it come from, when, how do you know it's true?"), Ben's
motive is unclear, and spreading it could do real harm. First, do no
harm (Hippocratic Oath). Passing along an unsubstantiated rumor would
make the manager a conduit for gossip, not a gatekeeper. The correct move
is to decline to amplify, seek to verify through legitimate channels if
action is genuinely warranted, and treat the claim as unconfirmed until
it is not.

## Key Takeaways

1. Be a gatekeeper, not a spy: never obtain information without
   permission, for another's benefit, or by deceit.
2. Anchor every decision in "first, do no harm" and "treat others as you
   would be treated."
3. Classify information as completely confidential, closed box, or open
   box before deciding how to handle it.
4. For closed-box information, broadcast that it exists and is
   progressing while withholding the details — silence breeds worst-case
   assumptions.
5. Apply the five-criteria test before sharing; if it fails, question why
   you want to tell them.
6. Engage in politics deliberately and for the greater good — map who is
   influential, build consensus early and informally, and be consistently
   yourself.
7. Avoid the negative moves — misuse of power, level-jumping, going rogue,
   personal gain — because they erode trust that seniority depends on.
