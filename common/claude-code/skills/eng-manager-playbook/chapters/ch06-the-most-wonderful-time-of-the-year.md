# Chapter 6: The Most Wonderful Time of the Year

## Core Idea

Performance reviews are painful only when done badly; run as a
well-prepared, two-way, no-surprises conversation — with pay decoupled
from performance — they become the best tool you have to push top
performers and course-correct everyone else.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Review Tracker**: A spreadsheet grid, one row per report, that
  keeps all the moving preparation parts visible.
  - When to use: Managing several reviews at once in the three weeks
    before review meetings.
  - How: Columns for Staff, Form shared, Mine filled in, Theirs filled
    in, Peer feedback 1–5 (red = not sent, yellow = requested, green =
    received), Meeting booked, Peer feedback collated, and Goals. Book
    meetings first by renaming existing one-to-one slots.

- **The Three-Part Written Review Form**: A shared document written and
  read before the meeting.
  - When to use: Every review, so the meeting has no surprises.
  - How: (1) Your observations on their achievements and development
    areas; (2) their observations on the same, plus reflection, future,
    and support; (3) mutually agreed goals for the next six months —
    seeded going in, discussed during, finalized after.

- **The Three-Equal-Thirds Meeting**: Split the review hour into equal
  parts.
  - When to use: On the day, once preparation is done.
  - How: One third reflective (the period just passed), one third
    forward-looking (the future), one third collaborative goal-drafting.
    In the first two thirds let them do the talking; do not step through
    the written form line by line.

- **Peer Feedback Gathering**: Solicit 3–5 varied perspectives per
  report by email.
  - When to use: Before each review, to give reports a fuller picture
    than your view alone.
  - How: Pick from teammates, cross-team collaborators, mentees, and
    different-skill colleagues (PMs, marketers, your boss). Ask four
    questions: what they excelled at, what to do differently and why,
    any other feedback, and whether to give it anonymously. Nudge once
    after a week; collate verbatim into a linked document.

## Key Concepts

- **No-surprises principle**: The pertinent information should already
  have been written, exchanged, and digested before the meeting — a
  review is a checkpoint, not a big reveal.
- **Decoupling pay from performance**: Deliver salary news at a separate
  time so money worry does not drown out the performance conversation.
- **Facilitator, not dictator**: In the review you serve the report and
  guide the conversation rather than hand down a verdict.
- **Six-month cadence**: Review twice a year (year-end plus mid-year);
  yearly is too infrequent, quarterly too often.
- **Development, not failures**: Frame all critique as development, even
  for struggling staff — the section is named for growth, not blame.
- **Peer feedback breadth**: Compose a picture from multiple angles;
  going broad with five people hedges against non-replies.
- **Keep the thought bubble over their head**: Prompt their reflections
  rather than supplying your own conclusions.

## Mental Models

- Think of the review meeting as one milestone in a continuous
  year-round conversation, not the only time performance is discussed.
- Use the five stages of receiving bad news — ignore, deny, blame
  others, assume responsibility, find a solution — to see why sharing
  the written review early matters: it lets people move through stages
  1–4 alone so the meeting can focus on stage 5.
- Treat review-writing as real work: put a ticket in the backlog for
  each one and budget about an hour per report.
- Think of reviews as most valuable for your best performers, not your
  weakest — high growth needs your time and energy the most.

## Anti-patterns

- **Top-down one-way debrief**: A review is not a parole-board verdict;
  make it two-way with mutual preparation.
- **Box-ticking because "it's just an HR thing"**: Do reviews for their
  own value — even do a mid-year one when the company mandates only
  annual.
- **Reserving reviews for underperformers**: Everyone deserves the
  reflection; your stars benefit most.
- **Rushing them because "people hate reviews"**: People hate them
  because they are done badly — engage fully with the same passion as a
  project.
- **The surprise-on-the-day review**: Dread and interrogation come from
  withholding; share writing beforehand.
- **Using the review to unveil a pay raise**: Money hijacks the
  performance discussion; separate it.
- **Making reviews the only place performance is discussed**: Talk about
  performance openly all year so nothing in the review is a shock.

## Worked Example

Three weeks out, the manager builds the tracker and books seven reviews
by renaming existing one-to-one slots into private rooms. For each
report they pick 3–5 peer reviewers spanning teammates, cross-team
collaborators, and a product manager, then email the four-question
request asking for replies within two weeks; cells go red to yellow. A
week later, unanswered ones get a single polite nudge. Thin one-line
replies get a follow-up asking the writer to expand. Meanwhile both the
manager and the report fill the shared three-part form directly, leaving
comments as they write. The report answers achievements, reflection,
development, future, and support; the manager answers achievements,
development, and peer-feedback themes (with the collated feedback linked
verbatim). Both read everything before meeting. On the day the manager
rereads the form, then runs the hour in equal thirds — reflect on the
period, look forward, and co-draft goals — asking leading questions like
"What made that achievement feel so proud?" and "How can I support that
development?", letting the report talk. Goals are seeded, not finished,
to be refined in later one-to-ones. Pay is deliberately not mentioned;
the raise is delivered separately, with prepared justification and an
open door for pushback.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start preparation about three weeks out and drive it with a tracker;
   book meetings first by reusing one-to-one slots.
2. Run reviews on a six-month cadence even if the company mandates only
   annual — the mid-year one stays pay-free and pure performance.
3. Write and exchange the review document before the meeting so there
   are zero surprises and the room can focus on solutions.
4. Gather 3–5 varied peer perspectives per report; nudge once, collate
   verbatim, and chase thin replies for substance.
5. Split the meeting into equal thirds and keep the thought bubble over
   their head — prompt, listen, facilitate.
6. Frame every critique as development, and give your best performers the
   most energy since they thrive on being pushed.
7. Decouple pay from performance: deliver salary news separately, with
   context, and leave the door open for pushback.
