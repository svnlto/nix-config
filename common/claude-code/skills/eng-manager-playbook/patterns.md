# Patterns & Techniques

Concrete, repeatable techniques from the book. Each is a practitioner
move: when to reach for it, how to run it, what it costs.

## Ch1: A New Adventure

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Build the three-view snapshot before concluding** | First week in a new management role, before your own view is reliable | Meet each report and your manager one-to-one, record their views plus your own, then overlay all three as a Venn diagram | Slower than acting immediately, but prevents wrong conclusions from an untrained week-one view |
| **Read divergence as a communication signal** | Sorting snapshot observations | Where views disagree tells you the direction communication has broken (up, down, or a false belief), not just a fact dispute | Requires honest input from all three parties to be accurate |
| **Protect makers' schedules when booking meetings** | Scheduling one-to-ones and meetings with engineers | Ask each report for their ideal time; batch meetings to preserve half-day focus blocks | Less convenient for you, but preserves team flow and output |

## Ch2: Manage Yourself First

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Run the day from the four tools** | Every working day | Open to-do list and calendar first thing, route all inputs into the four tools, batch email a few times a day, capture on the move and transcribe to the to-do list | Requires discipline to build the habit, but frees mental space and makes you reliable |
| **Extract action then archive email** | Processing any actionable email | Convert the action into a to-do item, then archive the email; never leave it as an implicit to-do in the inbox | One extra step per mail, but eliminates the top cause of forgotten commitments |
| **Categorize activities with Grove's buckets** | Reframing a scattered day or choosing how to spend time | Tag each activity as information gathering, decision-making, nudging, or being a role model | Adds reflective overhead, but recovers a sense of output and guides better time choices |
| **Judge time against the output equation** | Deciding whether to do a task yourself or delegate | Prefer actions that raise team output plus the output of those you influence over personal task completion | Feels less tangibly productive day-to-day, but maximizes real managerial output |

## Ch3: Interfacing with Humans

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Delegate as much as the needed control allows** | Handing off any task | Judge the person's expertise for that specific task and the task's criticality, place it on the delegation scale, retain accountability, transfer responsibility | More delegation means less control; accept that except for critical or highly visible work where you move left |
| **Match medium to message and recipient** | Before any consequential communication | Decide the message, put yourself in the recipient's shoes, then adapt to the platform (face-to-face, email, doc, chat) | More effort than mass email but lands better; suitability beats efficiency |
| **Weekly written summary to your manager** | Every week, ideally before your one-to-one | Spend thirty uninterrupted minutes writing Progress/Problems/Plans/People as a flow, proofread briefly, send via a commentable shared doc | Costs time but is therapeutic, surfaces solutions, and gives one-to-ones material |

## Ch4: One-to-Ones

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Contracting in the first one-to-one** | First meeting with each report; revisit as needs evolve | Send the five questions ahead, have both people answer each about the other, capture in the shared doc | Slightly formal but breaks the ice and prevents a bad start, especially when a peer becomes a report |
| **Keep it their meeting** | Every regular one-to-one | Ask leading questions, use silence, aim for 70% report talking, minimize status updates | Feels counterintuitive and takes practice; risks drifting into venting if unmanaged |
| **Note and action discipline** | During and after every one-to-one | Bullets with bold actions, review at end, put your own actions on your to-do via a recurring two-subtask reminder | Small overhead; failing to act on actions is a common trust-damaging failure |

## Ch5: The Right Job for the Person

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Craft the job around the person** | Any report seems mismatched to their current work | Diagnose their motivation and ZPD, then shape tasks, opportunities, and environment to fit rather than hunting for a mythical do-everything hire | Some person-team fits are impossible even with time; a move to another team may be the right answer |
| **Delegate as growth** | Assigning tasks across a team of mixed seniority | Give stretch tasks inside each person's ZPD; pair a junior with a senior so the same task grows both | Requires enough senior coverage; skill or seniority gaps leave some ZPD tasks unsupported |
| **Build a skill tree for distant goals** | A report names a big goal that feels like a pipe dream | Root the goal, split into subgoals, lay out ordered tangible milestones, start with the first achievable one, celebrate, repeat | One tree rarely reaches the summit; separate trees needed for parallel tracks (e.g. technical vs speaking) |
| **Name the cathedral/bazaar trait explicitly** | One-to-ones and upcoming project staffing | Ask whether each report is motivated by stability or change, then build it into delegation and team placement | Preconceptions are often wrong; traits shift with life circumstances, so revisit |

## Ch6: The Most Wonderful Time of the Year

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Prepare three weeks out with a tracker** | Managing several reviews at once | Build the tracker grid, book meetings by renaming one-to-one slots, then drive form-sharing and peer feedback to completion | Peer feedback always takes longer than expected; early start builds contingency for fires and vacations |
| **Write and exchange before meeting** | Every review | Both parties fill the shared three-part form and read it beforehand so the meeting has zero surprises | Costs ~1 hour of writing per report; budget it like real work with a backlog ticket |
| **Run the meeting in equal thirds** | On the day, prep done | Reflect, look forward, co-draft goals; ask leading questions and listen rather than reciting the form | Goals are seeded not finished; refined later in one-to-ones |
| **Decouple pay delivery** | Year-end reviews with salary attached | Deliver the raise separately with prepared justification and an open door for pushback | Some staff will still be dissatisfied and it's often out of your control; leave the door open |

## Ch7: Join Us

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Assess the whole team before opening a req** | You are granted headcount | Ask where the interdependent team bottlenecks (often QA or organization) and what the team itself would add before defaulting to another engineer. | Slower to post the advert, but avoids worsening an existing throughput bottleneck. |
| **Hire for potential over pedigree** | Writing adverts and interviewing | Assess how far a candidate can grow into the role, not just arriving skills; promote the opportunity to learn rather than a laundry list. | Requires investment in mentoring; rejects the fantasy of a perfect exact-fit hire. |
| **Over-describe every interview step** | Running any interview stage | State what's coming before doing it ('we'll do a whiteboard exercise, no right answer, we'll figure it out together'), allow for nerves, work problems together. | Takes more of the interviewer's talk time but yields a truer read of ability. |
| **Side with no when on the fence** | Deciding whether to progress or offer | Only advance or offer candidates you are sure will do a great job; collect evidence (answers, not judgments) and don't confer before independent verdicts. | May reject a borderline good hire, but protects the team from supporting an underperformer. |

## Ch8: Game Over

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Facilitate the good departure** | Someone leaves for good reasons | Agree an end date together, offer a reference, do a thorough handover, start hiring the replacement, and offer a standing invitation to return. | Costs handover time but preserves goodwill and boomerang hires. |
| **Surface zingers early in one-to-ones** | Ongoing team management | Build regular conversations about career progression, Maslow's hierarchy, and pet peeves into one-to-ones so simmering issues surface before they cause a surprise exit. | Requires consistent one-to-one discipline; can't catch every departure. |
| **Decide fight-or-release fast** | Notice lands | Fight only for top performers whose real catalyst you can satisfy; enlist your manager, HR, and peer managers; formulate a counteroffer and deliver in person plus email. | Keeping someone introduces imbalance (higher pay, others cover slack); fighting when you can't fix the cause only defers the exit. |
| **Run a fair PIP** | Continual performance conversations have failed | Document evidenced issues, set measurable time-boxed goals, fix a one-to-three-month window, clear with HR and manager, run weekly check-ins tied to the document, escalate to formal warning then dismissal. | Emotionally hard and legally sensitive, but a win-win: performance recovers or you get to rehire. |

## Ch9: How to Win Friends and Influence People

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Coach with the thought bubble overhead** | You already know the solution but want the person to build the skill of finding it | Ask open leading questions ('What's hard about it?', 'Have we solved this before?') until they hone in; be directive only if they'll never get there | Slower than telling; requires restraint, but teaches reusable problem-solving |
| **Run a GROW coaching session** | Structured one-off or recurring session on a specific problem | Walk Goal, Reality, Options, Wrap-up on a whiteboard, committing to a choice and support at the end | Adds structure that prevents drift; can feel formal for quick kitchen chats |
| **Contract the mentorship up front** | Starting any new mentoring relationship | Both answer the five questions (time, flexibility, confidentiality, boundaries, review, exit) in a shared doc before meeting | Up-front effort, but prevents boundary blur and unclear expectations |
| **Build a deliberately diverse network** | New to a company or newly promoted from IC | Ask peers/manager who they interact with and why; introduce yourself by email; span departments, locations, genders, seniorities | Takes concerted effort especially for introverts; pays back in balanced information |

## Ch10: Humans Are Hard

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Contain the wobble** | Charged, uncertain news from above could destabilize your team | Listen and separate fact from emotion; digest with time and sleep; reframe facts positively and communicate them; use peers to absorb your own hit | Delays delivery of news, but prevents catastrophizing rumor and morale damage |
| **Turn the whip into the carrot** | Someone important claims your team isn't working hard enough | Judge truthfulness (visible output, hustle, passion), argue if false or change conditions if true, and build autonomy/mastery/purpose | Long-term play requiring emotional intelligence, not a quick fix; but scared engineers just leave |
| **Manage Mount Stupid** | Confidence and competence seem mismatched in a person or room | Coach the overconfident to find the hard edges themselves, moving big-ego arguments to written facts; pair and draw out the impostor-afflicted | Requires reading the room and picking battles; sometimes you must leave and revisit facts later |
| **Work the emotion scale** | Your team scrutinizes you during a stressful period | Locate each person on kindness->mutiny, invest listening time in those near the bottom, and find the root cause (often not you) | Time-intensive; but resentment left alone curdles into mutiny |

## Ch11: Projects Are Hard

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Detect the Eye early** | Stakeholders suddenly probe, senior people appear, or a feature gets hyped beyond its original scope | Read the warning signs, increase vigilance, and start applying the Under the Gaze principles daily | Requires proactive attention; handled well it means career growth, handled poorly the next project goes elsewhere |
| **Investigate the deadline** | It looks like you will run out of time | Work with whoever set the deadline to learn what slipping actually costs; most dates move more than they appear to (even Apple ships 'in a few months' after WWDC reveals) | If the date is truly fixed you must instead pull scope or resources |
| **Use MoSCoW to make scope a lever** | Prioritizing a backlog against a deadline | Label features Must/Should/Could/Won't up front, plan milestones with buffers, and drop non-Musts when in a pinch; revisit categories as the project evolves | Up-front effort, but provides a safety net when things go wrong |
| **Engineer the fallow period** | Immediately after any crunch delivery | Celebrate and thank the team, spend a sprint clearing debt, allow self-guided learning, run a retro, plan next; push back on demands that steal the recovery window | Costs delivery time now but prevents burnout and attrition |

## Ch12: The Information Stock Exchange

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Classify before sharing** | Before deciding whether and how to share any sensitive information | Sort it into completely confidential, closed box, or open box, then apply the matching handling | Requires deliberate judgment but prevents both leaks and needless silence |
| **Broadcast the box, withhold the contents** | You hold closed-box information such as an in-progress pay review | Regularly update everyone that the process is progressing while keeping details undisclosed until finished | Slightly more communication effort, but silence otherwise breeds negative worst-case rumors |
| **Run the share-with test** | You feel compelled to tell someone something sensitive | Check all five criteria (relevance, reason, mutual benefit, minimal disclosure, trust); if any fail, question your motive | Curbs the human urge to share for rapport at the cost of confidentiality |
| **Build consensus early and informally** | Your team wants to do something dramatically different | Start with informal conversations with senior/influential people, then propose a POC PR or idea paper framed as a proposal, not a mandate | Slower than going rogue but avoids far worse downstream conflict |

## Ch13: Letting Go of Control

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Reframe worry with the trichotomy of control** | You cannot sleep for anxiety about a delegated task, project outcome, or someone's decision | Ask which of the three buckets the worry belongs to; if it is out of your hands, your only honest job is to have tried your best and set an internal goal | Requires tolerating uncertainty; not an excuse to stop caring or trying |
| **Carve out R-mode time and run at 85%** | You feel busy but uncreative, reactive, with no new insight | Block ~10% of the week with no agenda away from the desk, carry a capture tool, and plan commitments to 85% so slack absorbs emergencies | Must endure the guilt of unfilled time and possible perception of slacking |
| **Batch communication, protect sleep and movement** | Compulsive checking and long hours are degrading focus and health | Open email/chat only at a few set times; guard eight hours of sleep (cool room, no gadgets, fixed schedule, less late caffeine/alcohol); build movement into the day | Feels like missing out at first; requires breaking dopamine habits |

## Ch14: Good Housekeeping

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Break silos with guilds** | Teams reinvent the wheel, tech choices diverge, integrations surprise everyone, or engineers feel disconnected | Announce intentions and the sharing-to-delivery position, pick a balanced small core across teams, choose a leader and cadence, create open channels, decide documentation | No formal power means momentum depends on engagement; guilds may need disbanding, merging, or splitting over time |
| **Run a five-whys after every incident** | Any production incident, downtime, or contested status-quo assumption | Gather the team at a whiteboard right after the fix, chain why-questions from symptom to root cause, write up Q&A and outcome, create tickets, share the doc | Costs time before moving on; needs blameless framing to stay productive |
| **Grow a talks culture and document decisions** | Knowledge stays siloed and people lack speaking practice or design context | Start with five-minute lightning talks, graduate to department talks; capture design choices in ADRs; run quarterly health checks; name DRIs for orphaned areas | Requires prep time and sustained ownership; lightweight but must avoid overloaded slides or unused RACI-style overhead |

## Ch15: Dual Ladders

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Define both ladders explicitly** | Your company ties promotion solely to becoming a manager, or lacks a defined path | Publish IC and management levels lined up by experience; ensure comparable pay at comparable contribution and no glass ceiling | Requires senior-leadership buy-in; reveals whether the org has technical vs leadership pay bias |
| **Build the grid as a team workshop** | Staff lack a shared vocabulary for where they are and want to go | Roles across top, competencies down side, describe each cell together | Must stay a compass; discrete checkboxes invite gaming and are impossible to measure objectively |
| **Get-out clause for new managers** | A strong IC is nervous about the leap into management | 30-60-90-day plan plus explicit no-hard-feelings return to old role | Prevents Peter-Principle attrition; costs a formal handover and clear objectives |
| **Ratify, don't surprise, promotions** | Deciding when someone is ready to level up | Back promotions with accumulating peer feedback so they are self-evident to peers | If a senior promotion isn't self-evident to peers, you probably made the wrong call |

## Ch16: The Modern Workplace

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Live the three D&I principles daily** | In hiring, meetings, and everyday interactions | Remember your bias, call out incorrect behavior privately, champion inclusiveness | A new manager without formal authority must lean on their network to enact larger change |
| **Pitch a remote-work experiment** | The org resists remote work but staff want it | Offer one WFH day a week, own the output, shift to written/async comms, retrospect after a few months | Puts your reputation on the line; likely reveals people focus better and hiring can widen |
| **Treat every meeting as remote-inclusive** | You have any regular remote workers | Use a spotter, good AV, individual laptops over shared webcams, and written follow-ups | More setup effort; without it remote staff get less space to contribute |
| **Protect the team from hustle culture** | Industry norms push grind-at-all-costs | Cap sensible hours, kill out-of-hours dependency, leave loudly, and walk the walk yourself | Preaching balance while emailing at 11pm sets the opposite precedent |

## Ch17: Startups

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Target 'manager number one' openings** | A small team (low tens of people) has just raised seed money and no one owns engineering process. | Watch for small-team VP-engineering ads and network for unadvertised roles; step into ownership of process, hiring, delivery, and performance. | Accountability, risk, and autonomy turned up to 11 — you invent process where none exists; lower base pay than big companies. |
| **Approach startups by funding stage** | You want to enter or grow in management via a startup. | Co-founder stage: register interest through your network. Initial-investment stage: watch TechCrunch/Hacker News/AngelList/Product Hunt, reach out directly, ask for manager number one. VC rounds: standard process plus a cover letter stating management intent. | Most startups fail; intense work without big-company structure, comp, or benefits. |
| **Bring a management mindset as counter-balance** | A CTO and company push forward pedal-to-the-metal and chaos grows. | Guard process, efficiency, team organization, communication, quality, and organizational scalability with a light touch, not bureaucracy. | Requires tolerating pivots and ambiguity; be a bazaar browser, not only a cathedral builder. |

## Ch18: The Crystal Ball

| Technique | When | How | Trade-offs |
|-----------|------|-----|------------|
| **Backcast a vision into a one-year plan** | You have a vivid ten-year vision and need to know what to do this year. | For each horizon (5/3/2/1 years) jot the skills, opportunities, training, and networking required; specify concrete provable actions and define what success looks like for the year. | Must stay flexible — rigidly sticking to a ten-year plan makes you unhappy because life changes. |
| **Make the plan measurable via competencies and a backlog** | A plan risks staying abstract and untracked. | Rate yourself on career-ladder competencies, map each plan action to the competency it advances, record in Trello, review quarterly with your manager. | Requires honest self-assessment and outside peer input to correct blind spots. |
| **Coach staff through the exercise (5 steps)** | You want to be your reports' career coach, not just their manager. | Step 1 timeline homework; Step 2 ten-years coaching session (coach posture, provocative questions, summarize back); Step 3 review their vision for authenticity; Step 4 competency scoring plus peer feedback; Step 5 one-year session reconciling self-vs-peer gaps with radical candor and building a measurable backlog. | Ongoing commitment — revisit backlog every three months, refine plan every six. |
