# Cheatsheet

Decision rules distilled from the book — the judgment calls, stated so
you can act without re-reading. Grouped by chapter.

## Ch1: A New Adventure

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| you join a team | don't act in week one, build the three-view snapshot first | your own observations aren't reliable yet |
| introducing yourself | meet each report one-to-one in person or video | group hellos block honest, private disclosure |
| an observation appears in only two of the three views | classify it as a communication-direction problem | that tells you who to talk to |
| your action list is huge | prioritize by impact | an overwhelming list gets abandoned |
| you find issues in the snapshot | seed discussions not instant fixes | week one is for discovery |

## Ch2: Manage Yourself First

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| information arrives from anywhere | put it in one of the four tools | you can't run management from memory |
| you get an actionable email | extract the action to your to-do list then archive it | unread mail is the top cause of forgotten tasks |
| you need deep work | block 'no meetings please' time in the calendar | your calendar is yours to manage, not just others' |
| a day feels wasted | recategorize its moments into Grove's four buckets | even a kitchen chat is real managerial work |
| choosing how to spend time | apply output = team + those you influence | your team's output outweighs your personal output |
| tempted to preach a standard | model it first | leading by example is the way to create change |

## Ch3: Interfacing with Humans

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| giving feedback | care personally and challenge directly | doing one without the other becomes ruinous empathy or obnoxious aggression |
| you delegate | transfer responsibility but keep accountability | giving away both is abdication and work goes unmonitored |
| frustrated with delegated work | move the scale left rather than taking it back | snatching it cheats staff of learning and demoralizes them |
| you leave a frustrating meeting | name your mood up front or delay the next activity | nonverbal signals leak and you may misdirect anger |
| you have exciting-but-unconfirmed news | wait until it's confirmed | a manager's word carries weight and rumors erode trust |
| working with your manager | pull don't push and send a Four-Ps weekly summary | you own getting value from the relationship |

## Ch4: One-to-Ones

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| you start a new reporting relationship | run contracting in the first one-to-one | setting mutual expectations up front prevents an awkward or bad start |
| a report is quiet or you want depth | ask leading questions and let silence sit | they surface the issues that matter most |
| you feel the urge to solve their problem | ask another question instead | it's their meeting and they should reach the conclusion |
| a topic gets uncomfortable or off-topic | pull the Andon Cord | stopping and asking why beats pushing through |
| you sense distress or personal crisis | refer to HR or a professional and never diagnose | you are a manager, not a therapist |
| you assign yourself an action | put it on your to-do list | acting on one-to-one items builds trust many managers lose |

## Ch5: The Right Job for the Person

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| a well-paid report is disengaged | map them onto Maslow and invest in belonging/esteem/self-actualization | the company already covers basic needs |
| assigning a hard task | give it to a junior paired with a senior | it grows skill and mentoring at once inside the ZPD |
| a goal feels too distant to act on | draw a skill tree and start at the first milestone | incremental ZPD progress is measurable and motivating |
| staffing a stable long-term area | put a cathedral constructor on it not a bazaar browser | stability reads as mastery to one and stagnation to the other |
| a report underperforms | check whether they need more variety before assuming they need to stick with what they know | preconceptions about motivation are often wrong |

## Ch6: The Most Wonderful Time of the Year

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| several reviews loom | start three weeks out with a tracker and book by renaming one-to-one slots | peer feedback always runs late |
| writing the review | share it before the meeting | it lets people move through denial to solution alone, so the room focuses on step 5 |
| running the meeting | split it into equal thirds and let them talk | your job is to facilitate, not recite the form |
| gathering peer feedback | pick 3-5 varied people and nudge once | breadth hedges non-replies and one nudge is polite, more is annoying |
| a raise is due | deliver it separately from the review | money worry hijacks the performance conversation |
| the company mandates only annual reviews | do a mid-year one anyway | it gives a pay-free pure-performance checkpoint |

## Ch7: Join Us

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| granted headcount | assess where the interdependent team bottlenecks before hiring another engineer | output balance beats raw talent |
| writing an advert | publish the salary band and run the text through a gender-decoder tool | hidden bias and no-salary repel applicants, especially women |
| listing requirements | describe broad traits and example projects not a skills laundry list | women apply at ~100% match, men at ~60% |
| on the fence about a candidate | side with no | your team pays the cost of an underperformer |
| setting a take-home test | cap it at a couple of hours, scope it clearly, mirror real work | longer disrespects free time and makes them work for free |
| making an offer | balance peer pay, market rate, and impact, and justify it | lowballing is insulting after they invested time |

## Ch8: Game Over

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| someone gives notice for good reasons | facilitate it — agree an end date, offer a reference and a return path | goodwill brings people back |
| you get a 'zinger' | treat it as a communication failure and add career/Maslow/pet-peeve talks to one-to-ones | surprises come from unsurfaced issues |
| deciding to fight for staff | fight only for top performers whose catalyst you can actually satisfy | otherwise you just defer the departure and accept imbalance for nothing |
| performance is poor | address it directly and continually before any PIP | a surprise PIP is unfair and betrays trust |
| writing a PIP | use specific evidence and achievable time-boxed goals cleared by HR and your manager | unachievable PIPs are unethical and potentially unlawful |
| doing a layoff or firing | stick to the script, stay centered, do it yourself, and take space afterward | you owe staff your presence through the bad times |

## Ch9: How to Win Friends and Influence People

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| you know the answer but want growth | keep the thought bubble over their head and ask leading questions | it teaches reusable thinking |
| a coaching session risks drifting | run GROW | Goal-Reality-Options-Wrap-up keeps it on track |
| starting a mentorship | contract the five questions first | explicit boundaries prevent the mentor owning the mentee's deadlines |
| staff are less experienced | be more directive; as they mature, follow their interest more | pull develops autonomy |
| you need company-wide signal | connect with periphery staff | the snow melts at the periphery before the core sees it |
| you want to reach someone new | email out of the blue | a polite introduction almost never lands badly |
| you need help you can't get internally | ask the company for an external coach | sensitive senior problems are unsafe to share with peers or reports |

## Ch10: Humans Are Hard

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| charged news arrives from above | contain the wobble — listen, digest, reframe facts, never hide them | a prod at the top wobbles the whole org for weeks |
| told your team isn't working hard enough | judge it against visible output, hustle, and passion first | you either argue the case or change conditions |
| you want sustainable throughput | build autonomy, mastery, and purpose | scared engineers leave and motivated ones ship faster |
| you feel the urge to whip | don't — fake deadlines get seen through and drive people away | talent demand outstrips supply |
| a report resents you | dig for the root cause | you're usually the catalyst, not the cause |
| someone overconfident is on Mount Stupid | coach them to discover the difficulty and present only facts | flexing superior knowledge humiliates instead of teaching |
| a high achiever is shy | pair them and draw out their opinion directly | repeated visible success cures impostor syndrome |

## Ch11: Projects Are Hard

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| a project is slipping | do not add people to sequential work (because Brooks' Law makes it later); borrow an engineer only for genuinely parallelizable pieces. | — |
| under deadline pressure | adjust scope, resources, or time but never quality | shipping poor-quality software means you are in the wrong job |
| a deadline looks impossible | investigate what slipping actually costs first | most dates move more than stakeholders claim |
| in a scope pinch | commit only to the Musts and defer Shoulds/Coulds behind feature toggles | MoSCoW pre-decides your safety net |
| a crunch ends | fight for a fallow period of celebration, debt cleanup, and regrouping | stress after stress causes burnout |
| someone says the team should 'work harder' | reframe the conversation onto scope/resources/time trade-offs | effort is not the lever and invites ungrounded criticism |
| people claim engineering has slowed | explain productivity per head decreases with growth | it is structural, not individual performance |

## Ch12: The Information Stock Exchange

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| sent information you lack permission to see | tell the sender and delete it unopened | you must be a gatekeeper, not a spy |
| handling any information | apply 'first, do no harm' and the Golden Rule | they get you most of the way there |
| you hold closed-box info like a pay review | broadcast that it is progressing but withhold details | absence of information makes people assume the worst |
| tempted to share a secret | run the five-criteria share-with test | the human urge to share for rapport breaks confidentiality |
| handed an unverified rumor | decline to amplify and verify through legitimate channels | passing it on makes you a gossip conduit and can do harm |
| you must override a decision | spend your authority sparingly | the energy bar depletes and only replenishes by moving with the team |
| you disagree on a finalized decision | disagree and commit | unresolved dissent past the boundary blocks progress |

## Ch13: Letting Go of Control

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| anxious about an outcome you only partly control | set an internal goal 'I did my best' | external goals stake your happiness on what was never yours |
| drowning in reactive tasks with no insight | block ~10% agenda-free time away from the desk | R-mode only fires in quiet conditions |
| planning a sprint or week | commit to only 85% | emergencies are guaranteed and availability is a feature, not slack |
| tempted to keep email/chat open | close them and batch to a few times a day | engineered dopamine hits keep you reactive and low-impact |
| trading sleep for productivity | don't | sleep is a one-time offer you cannot recover on the weekend and it is the best legal performance enhancer |

## Ch14: Good Housekeeping

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| software looks fragmented | look first at how the teams talk, not just the code | of Conway's Law |
| teams reinvent work or diverge on tech | seed a guild | cross-team multicast breaks silos without a mandate |
| an incident is fixed | run a five-whys and share the write-up | the same bug recurs if you only patch the symptom |
| process pain is wider than one team | raise a management-bug ticket | open tracking builds momentum and is psychologically safer than going to leadership |
| you reach a design decision | write an ADR | future readers lose the reasoning otherwise, even after people leave |
| something keeps falling through the cracks | name a DRI | a single accountable name beats a heavy, forgotten RACI matrix |

## Ch15: Dual Ladders

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| your only promotion path is management | define a parallel IC ladder | forcing management drives good engineers out |
| building levels | line IC and management levels up by experience and pay them comparably | glass ceilings reveal leadership bias |
| a strong IC fears trying management | give a 30-60-90-day plan with a get-out clause | reversibility prevents Peter-Principle attrition |
| a promotion isn't self-evident to the person's peers | don't make it | promotion should ratify a position already almost reached |
| defining the grid | treat cells as a compass not checkboxes | discrete criteria are unmeasurable and gameable |
| splitting a fixed raise budget | fix underpayment first, then promotions, then cost-of-living, then performance | a flat percentage grows big salaries more than small ones |
| you code as a manager | stop if it costs your managerial duties | the track you're on defines your primary function |

## Ch16: The Modern Workplace

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| hiring | remember your bias and call out incorrect behavior privately | implicit bias reproduces homogeneous teams |
| you improve diversity | invest equally in inclusion | diversity without inclusion is just a numbers game |
| favoring an underrepresented candidate | use positive action not positive discrimination | the latter is illegal and ignores merit |
| the org resists remote work | pitch a one-day WFH experiment and own the output | evidence beats abstract argument |
| a remote report goes quiet | read it as focus not risk | poking out-of-sight staff is micromanagement |
| you have any remote workers | treat every meeting as remote-inclusive with a spotter and written follow-ups | in-room staff naturally crowd out remote voices |
| you preach work-life balance | walk the walk and leave loudly | misaligned words and actions set the wrong culture |

## Ch17: Startups

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| a small startup just took seed money and has no one owning engineering | apply for manager number one | a management vacuum is where you gain the whole department's scope fastest |
| only IC roles are advertised at a startup | state your management intent directly anyway | it opens doors the job board doesn't show |
| offered startup equity | treat it as a bonus and get legal advice on the terms | most startups fail and winners take 10+ years to pay out |
| deciding whether to join | confirm you are passionate about the product and comfortable with pivots | the ownership and volatility are multiplied by 100 |
| a CTO is racing ahead | apply light-touch management on process and quality | good management is the counter-balance to chaos, not bureaucracy |

## Ch18: The Crystal Ball

| When | Then | Why |
|------|------|-----|
| coaching a report's career | do your own vision-and-plan exercise first | you cannot guide an exercise you have not done |
| someone struggles to dream big | have them first plot ten years of past events | the past proves how much a decade can hold |
| writing a vision | use vivid first-person present tense | a vague vision has no magnetic pull |
| a plan feels abstract | map its actions to career-ladder competencies and a tracked backlog | that makes progress measurable |
| self-ratings and peer ratings diverge | reconcile the gap with radical candor | blind spots hide in the difference |
| maintaining a backlog | revisit it quarterly and refine the plan every six months | the vision is a direction, not a fixed destination |
