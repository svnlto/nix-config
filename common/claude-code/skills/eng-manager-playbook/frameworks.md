## Manage yourself first

Your output = your team's output + the output of those you influence (Grove).
Keep as little in your head as possible: run everything through four tools —
calendar (time only), to-do list (single home for tasks), email (archive,
never delete), and a capture device. Plan to only **85% capacity** so
guaranteed emergencies don't break you, and protect makers' half-day focus
blocks.

Start with a **snapshot**, not action. In week one, build three views of the
team — yours, your manager's, the team's — and overlay them. Where two of three
agree and one disagrees, you've found a communication-direction problem (up,
down) or a false belief, not just a fact dispute.

## People: one-to-ones, feedback, delegation

**One-to-ones are the highest-leverage recurring act.** It's *their* meeting:
aim for the report to talk **70%** of the time; keep the thought bubble over
their head with leading questions instead of solving for them. Run
**Contracting** (mutual-expectation questions) in the first session, keep a
shared Note-and-Action doc, and always action your own items — that's the trust
most managers lose.

**Feedback: Radical Candor** (Kim Scott) — care personally *and* challenge
directly; doing one without the other is ruinous empathy or obnoxious
aggression. **Delegate** by transferring responsibility while keeping
accountability; when frustrated, move down the Scale of Delegation rather than
snatching work back.

## Motivation and the right job

Craft the job to the person, not the reverse. The company covers Maslow's
bottom two needs (pay, security); you supply belonging, esteem,
self-actualization. Assign stretch work inside the **Zone of Proximal
Development** (hard alone, doable with a mentor). Sustainable throughput comes
from **autonomy, mastery, purpose** (Pink), never the whip — fake deadlines get
seen through.

## Hiring and departures

Hiring is your highest-leverage act. Optimize the interdependent team's
bottleneck, not cloning yourself. Publish salary bands, describe broad traits
not skill laundry lists (women apply at ~100% match, men at ~60%), cap
take-homes at ~2 hours, and when on the fence, side with no. **Departures are
normal**: facilitate good ones; a PIP comes only after continual direct
conversations, never as a surprise, with specific evidence and achievable goals
cleared by HR.

## Pressure and projects

Under a deadline, adjust **scope, resources, or time — never quality**. Don't
add people to sequential work (Brooks' Law). Use **MoSCoW** to make scope
negotiable, commit only to Musts, and engineer a **fallow period** to recover
after crunch.

## Information and influence

Information is currency — be a **gatekeeper, not a spy**. Classify what you hold
(confidential / closed-box / shareable); share *consistently* and *just
enough*; run the five-criteria share-with test before disclosing. When consensus
fails, **disagree and commit**. Multiply reach through mentoring (GROW) and a
wide company network.

## Org housekeeping and careers

Communication structure dictates software design (Conway's Law). Seed guilds,
run Five Whys on incidents, write ADRs, name a **DRI** for anything falling
through the cracks. Define parallel **dual ladders** (IC and management) paid
comparably, or good engineers leave to get promoted. Offer first-time managers
a 30-60-90-day plan with a get-out clause. Promotion ratifies a level already
nearly reached — never a gamble. Anchor your own career with a ten-year vision
and a tracked skills backlog, then coach reports through the same exercise.
